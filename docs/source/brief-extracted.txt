SakhiLine — Project Brief & Architecture
​Oct 18, 2026 · ​@shankar
SakhiLine is a voice assistant for ASHA health workers, built for Gnani AI's Great Indian AI
Internship Challenge, with submissions due ​Nov 10, 2026 .
What we're building
An ASHA worker taps one button after a home visit and talks; SakhiLine fills the visit form,
she confirms it, and it goes to her supervisor for approval.
She speaks the way she naturally does, in her own language mixed with English medical
terms: "Sunita ka BP check kiya, Hb 9 tha, TT ka doosra injection due hai agle hafte." Before
she starts, the assistant pulls the beneficiary's history, so she only adds what is new.
The assistant works through three channels, one per network situation:
Two more pieces complete the product:
Supervisor review. Submitted records land in an approval queue for her ANM or block
health officer, who approves them or sends comments back.
Built-in stress tester. SakhiLine generates hundreds of synthetic noisy calls, runs them
through its own pipeline, and publishes accuracy by language and noise level. Every
accuracy claim in the demo comes with a measured number.
Why we're building it
ASHA workers lose hours to paperwork after every round of home visits, and voice
removes most of it.

The problem. After visits, ASHAs fill registers and apps by hand, often in English-heavy
forms, then chase supervisors and mothers separately. Data entry competes with the
visits themselves.
The users. ASHA workers (primary), their supervisors such as the ANM or block health
officer (reviewers). Rural health workers are named in the contest's Real Deal award
brief.
Why voice. ASHAs already describe visits out loud, in their own language, mixing in
English medical terms. Speaking is faster than typing on a phone and works for
workers less comfortable with English forms.
Why Gnani. Gnani's models are trained on Indian telephonic audio, code-mixed speech
and Indian languages. That is exactly this user's input: noisy, multilingual, often over a
weak phone line.
Why it wins the contest. It is easy to explain in a 60-second video, emotional enough to
be shared, and backed by measured accuracy numbers instead of claims.
Constraints
The hardest constraints are the 23-day window, synthetic-data-only rules, and Evon not
being on Gnani's API.

How data flows
Every input, real or synthetic, enters through one voice gateway, so all five flows share the
same pipeline after it.
1. Live app (primary)
1. She taps the button and names the beneficiary. The app fetches the beneficiary's
history from the backend and shows it.
2. The app streams 16kHz microphone audio over a WebSocket to the voice gateway.
3. The gateway streams it into Prisma; partial transcripts appear on screen as she speaks.
4. The dialogue engine (an LLM with function calling) turns the transcript into form fields,
validated against a strict schema (Zod). Missing or unclear fields get a follow-up
question, never a guess.

5. Fields fill on screen live. Timbre speaks short prompts or the read-back if she wants
audio.
6. She confirms or taps to fix a field, then submits. The record goes to Postgres with
status pending review.
Target: under about 1.2 seconds from when she stops talking to the next prompt, which
needs streaming at every stage.
2. Offline record and sync
1. With weak or no data, the app records audio locally and queues it in the phone's
browser storage.
2. When the signal returns, the app uploads the queued recordings.
3. A sync worker runs each one through the same pipeline as a batch job.
4. The draft form comes back to the app; she reviews, fixes and submits as in flow 1.
3. Phone-call fallback
1. She calls a number. The telephony provider (Exotel or Twilio) streams 8kHz call audio to
the gateway.
2. Same Prisma → engine → Timbre loop, voice-only, with a spoken read-back: "Sunita, Hb
9, TT second dose next Tuesday — sahi hai?"
3. On confirmation the record is saved as pending review, and she can check it in the app
later.
4. Supervisor review
1. Submitted records appear in the supervisor's approval queue on the dashboard.
2. Rule-based flags, with thresholds set by the health department, push certain records
to the top of the queue. The AI never decides what is risky.
3. The supervisor approves, or sends a comment back to the ASHA, who fixes and
resubmits.
5. Stress test (the twist)
1. Evon, self-hosted and offline, writes hundreds of synthetic visit scenarios: a spoken
script per scenario plus the correct record as ground-truth JSON.
2. Timbre voices each script as an ASHA.
3. A noise degrader adds village noise, downsamples to 8kHz, applies phone-codec
compression and drops packets, at controlled levels.

4. The audio enters the same gateway as real traffic, through a simulated-caller
WebSocket.
5. The evaluator compares each extracted record with its ground truth field by field and
reports accuracy by language, code-mixing level and noise level, plus latency
percentiles.
Where the Gnani models are used
All three Gnani models have a real job: Prisma hears, Timbre speaks and generates test
voices, and Evon writes the test scenarios offline.
The real-time conversation logic runs on whichever hosted LLM is fastest, because Evon is
not on Gnani's API and self-hosting it for live calls would add latency and GPU cost.
The stress test uses Gnani's text-to-speech to generate test data for Gnani's speech-to-
text, so the system measures itself. That is the Jugaad Genius angle and the strongest
interview talking point.
